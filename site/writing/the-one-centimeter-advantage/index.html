The One-Centimeter Advantage

We want world-class engineering. So why do we look down on people working with machines?

Okay, this whole thing started while I was making a grilled cheese.

Thick slices of that nutty Italian bread from Walmart, provolone, pepper jack, sharp cheddar, and a lot of unsalted butter. I was pressing it down in the pan, thinking about how India wants semiconductor factories, EVs, bullet trains, robotics, all of it.

But tell a middle-class Indian family their kid wants to go to a trade school and suddenly we need a family meeting.

Like, who exactly do we think is going to install, maintain, repair, and improve all this stuff?

I’m an Indian student doing my master’s in ECE at Clarkson, so I’m part of the engineering-degree ecosystem I’m questioning. I’m not a journalist or an economist. This was a sandwich thought that kept getting longer.

How much ability are we wasting because we’ve already decided what a successful person should look and sound like?

We can build a semiconductor plant and still judge its technician by their clothes, haircut, skin colour, or accent. The infrastructure advances while the same social filters decide who gets heard.

Someone who understands a machine well enough to keep it running deserves a fair chance to help improve it. That means access to people, equipment, and money to test an idea.

The technician already has dignity. The question is whether the system gives their ability somewhere to go.

People are our biggest resource. What are we doing with that?

I think labour, and the human capability behind it, is the most powerful resource a country has. People build, repair, organise, care, and figure things out. But their effort needs health, training, tools, reliable electricity, transport, and institutions that work.

A young population gives India an opportunity. It doesn’t deliver the result. The demographic dividend depends on productive opportunities for people entering working age. World Bank, jobs and the demographic dividend

Look at Japan. Using 2023 figures for both countries, India and Japan respectively had:

  • Population aged 65 and over: 6.9% and 29.6%.
  • Output per employed person: about 22,600 and 87,500 in constant 2021 PPP international dollars.
  • Output per person: about 8,900 and 47,400 on the same PPP basis.
  • Life expectancy: 72.0 and 84.7 years.
  • Human Development Index: 0.685 and 0.925.

The output and age figures come from the World Bank; health and HDI from UNDP’s 2025 report. PPP adjusts for price differences; these aren’t wages or market-exchange-rate dollar incomes. World Bank: age structure; output per employed person; output per person; UNDP statistical table.

Japan’s output per worker was roughly 3.9 times India’s. That reflects capital, technology, skills, industry mix, organisation, and working hours—not inherent superiority. Give the same technician dependable equipment, electricity, and payments, and their productive possibilities change.

The countries have different histories; this comparison cannot isolate prejudice as the cause. India is improving too: its HDI rose from 0.676 in 2022 to 0.685 in 2023. UNDP, India’s human development progress

Still, youth cannot replace functioning systems. Japan’s older people also contribute through work, care, and mentorship. Our ambition should be better productivity, pay, health, and lives. Cheap labour and employment counts alone don’t establish progress; job quality and unpaid care matter too.

The same work, with a different label

“My daughter works in electric mobility.” Sounds good.

“My daughter is training as a mechanic.” Suddenly it sounds different to some people.

Parents have legitimate concerns about wages, safety, training, and progression. Their kid has to live that life. But “what will relatives think?” and “what kind of people will she be around?” deserve scrutiny too.

Class, caste, colourism, and language prejudice are distinct but can overlap. “They don’t seem polished” can hide what actually bothered someone. None of those surface judgements establishes whether a person can diagnose a fault or run a business.

In an Indian hiring audit, Zahra Siddique found lower-caste applicants needed about 20% more applications for the same number of callbacks as higher-caste applicants. First published as a working paper in 2008, it’s specific, older evidence, not today’s national rate. Siddique, Caste Based Discrimination: Evidence and Policy

Fewer interviews, introductions, and customers can leave useful ability outside the room. Some barriers involve unfamiliar conventions; others involve discrimination. Teaching someone to write a quotation won’t fix a customer who rejects their caste or accent.

And an affluent manager who humiliates staff needs social education too. Good English doesn’t settle that.

What if a handful of people made trade schools feel normal?

Imagine educated, middle-class young people choosing precision machining, industrial automation, or EV diagnostics. People their friends recognise as the “modern Indian” type: familiar clothes, social confidence, the same cafés and interests.

Their friends see them having an ordinary life and doing skilled work the group previously dismissed. Wouldn’t some reconsider?

A familiar person can provide career information and reduce the perceived social penalty of that choice. A trusted former student or neighbour may matter more than an influencer.

A handful could open the conversation across a much larger circle. Turning that interest into lasting participation requires good training and careers.

This is a hypothesis worth testing. A 2025 German role-model study found small increases in aspirations for the occupations presented. It didn’t establish subsequent enrolment or employment, or test this specific mechanism in Indian ITIs. Böhle, Beckmann, and Granato, Preparing Transitions

And a trade shouldn’t need middle-class approval to deserve respect. Make the experienced technician teaching the newcomer visible too. Treat existing students as peers. If prestige rises, preserve affordable access and expand capacity where justified, so poorer students benefit instead of getting pushed out.

ITIs need the seriousness we give IITs

Industrial Training Institutes and other trade schools deserve competent teaching, functioning equipment, safe campuses, and credible progression. Their missions differ from IITs and NITs, so identical spending or publication targets would make little sense. The standard should be equally serious care for students’ learning.

What would that involve?

  • Usable workshops: fund maintenance, calibration, software, electricity, consumables, and repairs. Report supervised hands-on hours and equipment availability. A locked machine makes a nice photo and a bad education.
  • Strong instructors and relevant courses: fill vacancies, support industry attachments, and bring practitioners into teaching. Build foundations in electrical systems, machining, welding, safety, and measurement, with new technology suited to local demand.
  • Real apprenticeships and progression: paid learning with supervisors, protected training time, assessed competence, and transferable skills. Connect qualifications to advanced certificates, diplomas, and appropriate degree routes.
  • Practical access: transport or hostels where needed, usable toilets, disability access, childcare support where relevant, protection from harassment, and language and mathematics support. Private providers should demonstrate quality too.

Government has recognised the problem. PM-SETU provides for ₹60,000 crore over five years, split between the Centre (₹30,000 crore), states (₹20,000 crore), and industry (₹10,000 crore), including upgrades to 1,000 government ITIs and strengthening five National Skill Training Institutes. PM-SETU scheme guidelines; government operationalisation statement, March 2026.

Now ask what arrives, what works, who teaches, and what happens to students afterwards. Announcing investment starts the work.

And yes, question the IIT and NIT hype

Getting admitted is an achievement. The bullshit starts when we treat the credential as lifelong superiority. An ITI graduate should be able to challenge an engineer’s unsafe design and have the evidence taken seriously.

Institutions need scrutiny too. The 2026–27 Union Budget provides ₹12,123 crore for IIT support and ₹6,260 crore for NITs and IIEST under specified heads. These are estimates, including loan-servicing provisions, not audited expenditure or complete public costs. Union Budget, Department of Higher Education, items 38–39 and 44

Those annual figures aren’t directly comparable with PM-SETU’s five-year outlay. Proper comparisons need consistent years, actual spending, student numbers, research responsibilities, and state funding. Our argument should survive honest accounting.

The CAG’s 2021 audit of eight IITs established in 2008–09 found infrastructure delays, faculty shortages, and oversight weaknesses during 2014–19. That’s evidence about those institutions and years, not every IIT today or any NIT. CAG, Report No. 20 of 2021

Equally, the indigenous 5G testbed developed by a consortium including IITs, IISc, and other organisations demonstrates real shared capability. We can recognise it and still measure use, adoption, and public benefit. Indigenous 5G Testbed

How much capability does public investment create beyond the people who get admitted?

Salaries, valuations, patents, and famous alumni don’t answer that alone. Selection and prior preparation affect graduate success too. Accountability belongs to institutions and funding policy; graduates aren’t obliged to take particular jobs or remain in India. Fundamental research, open knowledge, and researcher training also deserve support over appropriate timescales.

What can we learn from the US and Europe?

Look at how institutions connect, beyond the university rankings.

MIT’s Technology Licensing Office reported 137 licences and options and 38 startups formed in FY2025. Those are reported activities, not a complete social-return calculation. They offer a starting point for transparent reporting followed by evidence of adoption. MIT TLO FY2025 report

The US National Science Foundation’s Advanced Technological Education programme gives two-year institutions a significant leadership role in partnerships with industry, universities, and schools. Technician education belongs inside the advanced-technology conversation. Those colleges aren’t identical to ITIs. NSF ATE programme

German universities of applied sciences offer practice-oriented degrees and industry connections. They suggest stronger applied routes and progression, rather than a direct ITI equivalent. DAAD, universities of applied sciences

Fraunhofer, a non-university applied-research organisation, combines public base funding with competitively acquired public and industrial project funding. It’s worth examining how that connects research to use, without imposing its funding model everywhere. NSF also assesses proposals on intellectual merit and broader impacts. Fraunhofer financing; NSF merit review.

These systems have inequalities too, and Germany doesn’t represent all of Europe. Numerical comparisons need budgets, institutional age, research fields, doctoral scale, laboratories, and industrial context. The useful question is whether each institution makes the surrounding system stronger.

Somewhere in the middle of thinking through all this, my second sandwich went cold. I’ll reheat it after I finish the thought. Apparently, we’re doing international education comparisons now.

Show us outcomes at a scale that matters

NIRF already includes research, graduation, placement, higher-study, and median-salary measures. Build on that with public five-year improvement agreements: baselines, funded plans, annual reporting, and independent reviews. NIRF, India Rankings 2025 report

Publish evidence suited to each mission:

  • Learning: assessed competence, completion, and progression for the full entering cohort, including dropouts.
  • Livelihoods: employment and study at 6, 12, and 24 months; real earnings and their distribution, hours, safety, and relevance. Include self-employment. Offers aren’t sustained jobs.
  • Usefulness: technologies actually used, repeat users, deployment duration, and verified improvements in cost, reliability, quality, safety, or public services.
  • Reach and cost: outside laboratory access, instructor learning, firms supported as a share of the relevant population, actual spending, costs per sustained outcome, and access across social groups.

At national scale, show underserved districts, coverage, missing data, and whether gains persist beyond leading campuses. Protect privacy, account for student backgrounds and local conditions, use credible comparisons where feasible, and avoid double-counting benefits.

Research quality, doctoral training, open tools, standards, and public-interest work need assessment too. Repeated failure should trigger corrective plans and leadership or funding review while protecting current students, essential teaching, and long-term research.

Start with funded regional partnerships between IITs/NITs, polytechnics, ITIs, firms, and public-service organisations. Specify testing access, instructor development, adopted improvements, and good employment. Give vocational partners a say.

And cost our proposal properly: recurring staffing, maintenance, responsible agencies, existing funding, and additional needs. Training alone won’t create demand. Employers must invest, offer meaningful apprenticeships, and pay for skills; courses and retraining should follow changing regional needs.

Let people learn the rules—and learn from each other

Some people grow up knowing how to deal with customers, write invoices, or challenge a supervisor. Others get judged while figuring it out. Teach useful conventions clearly and apply them to everyone.

Securing hair around machinery has a reason. Requiring a fashionable haircut doesn’t. Teach affordable hygiene, safety, boundaries, communication, and shared-space etiquette without treating someone’s background as something to erase. Provide the water, changing facilities, and equipment those standards require.

MSDE’s 2022–23 report already describes 120 hours of employability skills in one-year ITI courses and the first year of two-year courses, plus 60 advanced hours in year two. Topics include communication, inclusion, citizenship, financial/legal literacy, entrepreneurship, and customer service. The task is making that learning useful in practice. MSDE annual report, curriculum reforms

Practise explaining repairs, preparing quotations, reporting unsafe work, and recognising harassment, in accessible languages with job-relevant English support. Meet regularly over a semester, work on something together, explain mistakes privately, make useful introductions, and share credit. Mentorship should allow both people to learn.

That’s also why the class mix matters. Shared workshops bring different experiences and networks together. One student recognises a recurring fault; another documents it; another designs a test or finds a customer.

Discover strengths in individuals. The working-class student might be the best programmer; the middle-class student might need help with everything. Rotate responsibilities so confident English doesn’t automatically confer management authority.

Gautam Rao’s 2019 Delhi-school study found that poorer classmates made wealthier pupils less likely to discriminate and more willing to socialise with them. It supports taking sustained interaction seriously, while schoolchildren differ from adult apprentices. Rao, Familiarity Does Not Breed Contempt

Separately, a 2024 research account reported a 13.5% productivity increase from randomised soft-skills training among Indian garment-factory machine operators. That specific result supports attention to interpersonal skills, not a national GDP estimate for this proposal. Authors’ research account, NBER

Test a funded pilot across varied ITIs and employers: coordinators, travel support, accessible facilities, exploratory workshops, parent visits, and honest career information. Separate role-model exposure from sustained shared projects when evaluating effects.

Track interest through enrolment, completion, employment, earnings, and progression. Do peers follow early entrants? Do existing students gain connections? Examine outcomes for women and excluded groups, discrimination, and affordability, using comparable institutions where possible.

Outside class, friendship can be ordinary. Invite someone because you like them, choose places they can afford, and avoid making them a permanent self-improvement project.

The one-centimeter advantage

A technician stands one centimeter from the loose connector, damaged seal, or calibration problem—where a design meets heat, dust, vibration, unreliable power, and actual users.

A robotics technician might notice repeated installation errors; an EV mechanic might spot a diagnostic shortcut worth testing. This illustrates what Eric von Hippel calls “sticky information”: knowledge costly to acquire and transfer, making collaboration with the people holding it valuable. Von Hippel, “Sticky Information” and the Locus of Problem Solving

An engineer’s analysis and a technician’s field knowledge can complement each other. Downstream innovation is improvement emerging where technology gets installed, maintained, adapted, and used.

India already has practical inventors outside elite institutions. The National Innovation Foundation documents Mansukhbhai Jagani’s Bullet Santi, adapting a motorcycle into a multipurpose agricultural machine. It demonstrates capability, not proof that my mentorship proposal works. National Innovation Foundation, first national awards book

The route might be: document a recurring fault, test an improvement, find a paying customer, then make the service repeatable. Each step needs time, equipment, trust, working capital, contracts, and timely payments. Establish ownership and credit early so better-connected people don’t capture someone else’s insight.

Some improvements could support calibration, tooling, maintenance, or specialist engineering firms. Others could make existing employees more productive and better paid. Nobody needs to become a founder to count. Equipment finance or working-capital credit may suit a business better than venture capital.

Germany’s Mittelstand, defined by IfM Bonn through connected ownership and management, and its dual vocational system linking employers with schooling offer useful reference points. They aren’t a template for every Indian region. IfM Bonn, Mittelstand overview; BIBB, the dual system.

Also, existing community networks supply credit, insurance, training, and customers. Research on caste and occupational allocation shows that reducing barriers can improve talent allocation while weakening valuable network support. Wider access needs dependable alternatives, so people don’t lose their safety net. Cassan, Keniston, and Kleineberg, A Division of Laborers

Okay, what about $35 trillion?

India’s $30–35 trillion economy by 2047 is a stated government aspiration. Inclusion belongs alongside investment, energy, competitive firms, health, education, and institutional reform—not as a proven shortcut to that number. Government statement, Press Information Bureau

Starting from a rounded $4 trillion in 2025, close to the World Bank value retrieved for this draft, reaching $35 trillion over 22 years requires about 10.36% annual nominal dollar growth. That includes real output, domestic prices, and exchange-rate changes. World Bank GDP data

For perspective, $15–18 trillion would require 6.19–7.08%, and $24–28 trillion would require 8.49–9.25%. These are arithmetic illustrations. We cannot assign those outcomes to degrees of inclusion: no cited study establishes that causal link.

Exchange rates matter enormously. Holding annual real growth at 9% and GDP-deflator inflation at 4%, the same starting point gives roughly $40.8 trillion with 2% annual rupee depreciation, versus $32.9 trillion with 3%. Those assumptions aren’t forecasts or independently adjustable policy switches.

The World Bank separately estimates that high-income status by 2047 requires roughly 7.8% average real growth over its projection horizon, alongside investment, participation, and productivity gains. That classification concerns income per person, not a $35 trillion GDP forecast. World Bank, Becoming a High-Income Economy in a Generation

Inclusion could improve job matching, participation, learning, and the spread of useful methods. These affect different inputs and productivity; we shouldn’t call everything TFP or count it twice. Moving a graduate into an unsuitable trade creates no automatic gain.

Small sustained gains matter: an additional 1% annual productivity growth compounds to about 24.5% over 22 years, holding other inputs fixed. That illustrates persistence, not this proposal’s estimated effect.

Judge development by real incomes, health, learning, productivity, and access to opportunity too. Those make the headline mean something.

So what can we do?

Bring the person maintaining equipment into the design conversation. Let candidates demonstrate skill. Introduce a capable tradesperson to a customer and pay them on time. Explain unfamiliar conventions without humiliation.

If you want to learn a trade, investigate the training, pay, safety, and progression seriously. Let people see your choice, credit your teachers, and bring classmates into opportunities your network opens.

Give vocational students proper institutions. Ask celebrated campuses to demonstrate wider benefits. And let people belong socially without presenting an economic justification. Someone doesn’t need a startup idea to deserve a place at the table.

This isn’t the entire answer to development. But if people are our biggest resource, we should give more of their ability somewhere to go.

Somewhere, a technician is standing one centimeter from a problem we haven’t noticed. They might have an idea worth hearing.
