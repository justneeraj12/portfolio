# The White-Collar Mirage

*What happens to the students who win the engineering-degree race?*

## The offer letter tells the truth badly

An offer letter says **₹6 lakh CTC**. At home, that sounds like a salary. In a Bengaluru PG, it has to pay rent. So what actually reaches the bank account each month?

Take this *illustrative* offer. Of the ₹6 lakh headline, ₹5 lakh is fixed annual cash. ₹60,000 is variable pay that may not arrive. The employer's PF contribution adds ₹28,800 to the headline, and another ₹11,200 is provisioned for gratuity.

The monthly fixed gross is about ₹41,667. After an assumed ₹2,400 employee PF contribution and Karnataka's usual ₹200 professional tax, the bank credit is about **₹39,067 before income-tax withholding**. February's professional tax is ₹300.<sup><a id="note-ref-offer-1" href="#note-offer" aria-label="Methodology note 1">1</a></sup> [EPFO contribution rules](https://www.epfindia.gov.in/site_docs/PDFs/MiscPDFs/ContributionRate.pdf); [Karnataka professional-tax amendment](https://gst.karnataka.gov.in/Documents/General/ptnotificationact29323.pdf)

PF is the worker's asset, not money disappearing into a corporate pocket. Gratuity is a legal benefit, not imaginary money. But neither pays this month's rent. Put all four on the same line and call the result *salary*, and the arithmetic becomes a sales pitch.

In [The One-Centimeter Advantage](https://justneeraj12.github.io/portfolio/writing/the-one-centimeter-advantage/), I asked why we push young Indians away from skilled trades and towards engineering degrees as though a lanyard confers dignity. This is the other side of the same question. What happens when the kid gets the degree, clears the interview, moves to Bengaluru, and finds that the credential gives them very little control over their work?

We promised a route out of informal bargaining. Sometimes we have just put that bargaining inside an air-conditioned office.

## A hard filter for an ordinary seat

The easy answer is that India has too many engineering graduates. There is real competition for entry-level jobs. I would be an idiot to pretend otherwise. But “oversupply” cannot explain every choice an employer makes after deciding which graduates are good enough to interview.

One [2025 campus notice](https://hub.rgukt.ac.in/hub/tnp/index/9) at RGUKT Basar initially opened a software-engineering drive to students with **8.5 CGPA and above**. A later notice opened it to the 7.0–8.4 group. The listed full-time CTCs were ₹6 lakh and ₹7 lakh for associate frontend and backend roles and ₹9 lakh for R&D. One drive cannot describe the whole market. It can show how tight the gate gets for an ordinary pay band.<sup><a id="note-ref-screen-1" href="#note-screen" aria-label="Methodology note 2">2</a></sup>

I have nothing against a difficult interview when the difficulty measures the work. If the job involves algorithm design, test algorithm design. If it involves connecting APIs, diagnosing a failed deployment, talking to a client, and keeping a service running, ask a candidate to do *that*.

A high GPA and a puzzle can be useful signals. They are lousy substitutes for checking whether someone can perform the job. We spend years telling students to prove their merit, then sometimes measure the wrong thing at the last gate.

Campus rules can narrow the market further. [Delhi Technological University's 2025–26 placement policy](https://tnp.dtu.ac.in/docs/policy.pdf) generally lets a student with an offer of X CTC pursue a second campus offer only at **1.5 times X**, and only after 80% of the batch has been placed. [NIT Raipur's 2025–26 policy](https://cdc.nitrr.ac.in/docs/Placement%20Policy%202025-26.pdf) uses “one student, one job” for on-campus selection. I understand why colleges want offers spread across a batch. The cost is that a student who accepts an early, mediocre offer loses some ability to make employers compete for them.<sup><a id="note-ref-campus-1" href="#note-campus" aria-label="Methodology note 3">3</a></sup>

That is where the market starts to look strange. We ask for merit, then limit what merit can negotiate. We tell the graduate to be grateful for a job, then act as though wanting a better offer is disloyal.

Economists have a term for the power an employer gains when workers cannot easily take their labour elsewhere: *monopsony power*. You do not need literally one company in Bengaluru for it. Search costs, hiring gates, opaque pay, and penalties for leaving can all make the next offer harder to reach. [OECD, *Employment Outlook 2022*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2022/09/oecd-employment-outlook-2022_7a5a73b3/full-report/component-7.html)

Similar starting salaries alone do not prove a cartel. Wage-fixing or no-poach agreements would need evidence of coordination, and India's [Competition Commission](https://www.cci.gov.in/antitrust) should investigate if that evidence appears. We do not need to invent a secret meeting to see the problem in front of us: the rules around hiring can tilt bargaining power.

## The invoice and the payslip

Now follow the worker into a client project. The customer sees a service invoice. The worker sees a salary slip. The two numbers rarely sit on the same desk.

For a separate example, suppose an overseas client is billed **$40 an hour** for 1,700 hours of one person's work. At an illustrative ₹85 per dollar, that is ₹57.8 lakh for the year. At $80 an hour, it is ₹1.156 crore. If this worker receives ₹6 lakh in annual cash, that is roughly **10.4% or 5.2%** of those invoice amounts. This is a thought experiment, not a verified billing rate or a finding about any company.<sup><a id="note-ref-billing-1" href="#note-billing" aria-label="Methodology note 4">4</a></sup>

There are real costs between an invoice and a payslip: sales teams, software, training, benches, management, taxes, bad debts, and the risk of finding the next client. Count them. Also count the worker who does the work. The gap is not automatically profit, but it is a perfectly reasonable thing to examine.

And here is why I would not turn the thought experiment into an industry accusation. [Infosys reports](https://www.infosys.com/investors/reports-filings/financials/data-sheet.html) aggregate employee costs of **52.7% of revenue in FY2025 and 54.0% in FY2026**. Those accounts cannot show what one junior consultant received from one client contract. They also do not support saying that Indian IT firms as a whole give workers five to eight percent of revenue.<sup><a id="note-ref-billing-2" href="#note-billing" aria-label="Methodology note 4">4</a></sup>

What would worry me is a contract sold as one thing and staffed as another. Imagine a time-and-materials contract billed for fifteen full-time people while ten named people work on it. You would need the invoice and staffing records before calling that fraud. I am not claiming it is routine practice.

A fixed-price contract is different. Ten people delivering what fifteen were budgeted for could be a real efficiency gain. If those ten have to work twelve-hour days to make it happen, I want to know whether their hours were recorded and paid. The contract changes the accusation. It does not make the late-night work vanish.

This is where “the client needs it” becomes a dangerous sentence. A client request at 10 p.m. may be urgent. It is still work. If the employer sells the availability and the employee supplies it, the employer cannot treat the hours as a free gift from the employee's ambition.

## ₹8 lakh should be ordinary in Bengaluru

For an entry-level software or consulting job in Bengaluru that demands a degree, hard selection, and client-ready work, **₹8 lakh or more in guaranteed annual gross cash** should become a normal starting offer. I mean cash the contract promises, not ₹8 lakh CTC padded with a possible bonus and employer contributions. This is the standard I want the market to reach, not a claim about today's citywide average or a statutory minimum for every job.

That is about ₹66,667 a month in gross fixed pay. Under the same employee-PF and professional-tax assumptions as the offer-letter illustration, the monthly credit would be about **₹64,067 before income-tax withholding**, compared with ₹39,067 from the ₹5 lakh fixed-cash offer. The exact payslip will differ. The point is that “₹8 lakh salary” should mean something a worker can actually plan around.<sup><a id="note-ref-offer-2" href="#note-offer" aria-label="Methodology note 1">1</a></sup>

This is not some number imported from San Francisco. [IIIT Bangalore's recruiter guide for 2026–27 graduates](https://www.iiitb.ac.in/includefiles/userfiles/files/Guidelines%20for%20Recruiters%202026-27%20Graduates%281%29.pdf) sets **₹8 lakh in base compensation** as its minimum threshold for CSE and ECE offers in its campus process. IIIT-B is selective, and its guide cannot tell us what every Bengaluru graduate earns. It shows that this base-pay figure already exists in the city's hiring conversation.<sup><a id="note-ref-screen-2" href="#note-screen" aria-label="Methodology note 2">2</a></sup>

Now run the client numbers. For the *separate* worker in our hypothetical $40-an-hour contract, moving from ₹6 lakh to ₹8 lakh cash adds **₹2 lakh a year**. Across 1,700 billed hours at the illustrative ₹85 exchange rate, that is about **$1.38 per hour** if the firm passes the whole direct wage increase to the client. The $40 rate becomes $41.38, roughly **3.5% higher**. At $80, it becomes $81.38, roughly **1.7% higher**.<sup><a id="note-ref-billing-3" href="#note-billing" aria-label="Methodology note 4">4</a></sup>

The offer letter at the start has a lower baseline: ₹5 lakh in fixed cash. Moving *that* to ₹8 lakh costs ₹3 lakh more annually. Under the same hypothetical billing assumptions, full direct pass-through adds **$2.08 an hour**, taking $40 to $42.08, or **5.2% higher**. These are two starting salaries, not two claims about one real contract.<sup><a id="note-ref-billing-4" href="#note-billing" aria-label="Methodology note 4">4</a></sup>

That is a price conversation, not a threat to civilisation. A client may still push back. A firm could absorb part of the increase, rework a contract, or win work by being better. Employer PF and other costs mean the full increase may be somewhat larger than the direct-cash calculation. But “we cannot possibly pay ₹8 lakh” is a claim that deserves a ledger, not automatic respect.

And India is selling more than a discount. The [World Bank's digital progress report](https://ppp.worldbank.org/sites/default/files/2026-01/Digital%20Progress%20and%20Trends%20Report%202025%2C%20Strengthening%20AI%20Foundations.pdf) puts India at **11% of global IT-services exports in 2023**. [Nasscom's FY2025 review](https://www.nasscom.in/sites/default/files/media_pdf/Nasscom%20SR%20Press%20release.pdf) describes growth in engineering R&D, digital engineering, and more than 1,750 global capability centres. Those figures do not tell us what a particular client will pay. They do tell us this is an established industry with people doing more than cheap back-office work.<sup><a id="note-ref-scale-2" href="#note-scale" aria-label="Methodology note 7">7</a></sup>

The person on the call can build the service, explain a failure, work across time zones, and learn the next tool. Some are fresh graduates; some have been doing it for years. “Well spoken” should mean they can communicate the work clearly, not that they have copied a London accent. We are hiring educated, current, globally capable people. Why are we still talking as though the only thing we can sell is how little we pay them?

I am not promising a free lunch. Some contracts are price-sensitive. The proposed 35% pay floor later in this essay would be a much bigger change for a seat like our example; this ₹8 lakh calculation does not prove that separate proposal will work everywhere. That is why it needs a pilot and an audit. But if a company says *any* better wage will destroy its global business, **show the numbers**. The argument should survive a spreadsheet.

## A title is not an overtime exemption

Karnataka's [Shops and Commercial Establishments Act](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/7609/1/8_of_1962_%28e%29.pdf) already gives covered employees **twice normal wages** when they work beyond **nine hours a day or 48 hours a week**. People actually in management positions are exempt. A 22-year-old does not become management because a business card says *Consultant* or *Executive*.<sup><a id="note-ref-overtime-1" href="#note-overtime" aria-label="Methodology note 5">5</a></sup>

IT establishments have some special exemptions under the Karnataka Act, but not a blanket exemption from its overtime section. The national [Code on Wages](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/indiacode/handle/123456789/15793?view_type=browse) also provides double-rate overtime for employees whose minimum wage rate has been fixed under that Code; the labour ministry's [2026 FAQ](https://www.labour.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/03/a4ccf4c6d97c4f1f36a6d83f8c64213d.pdf?v=20260620082854) says eligible employees can include supervisory and managerial staff.<sup><a id="note-ref-overtime-2" href="#note-overtime" aria-label="Methodology note 5">5</a></sup>

So “office workers have no protection” is too easy an excuse. The real question is which rule covers this worker, and how the worker proves the hours.

That is where the argument gets annoyingly practical. A manager says the VPN was merely open. The worker says the ticket took three hours. Teams shows a green dot, Jira shows two updates, and nobody has a reliable account of the time between them.

None of those records alone proves a wage claim. Put assignments, messages, time sheets, client billing, and the worker's account together, and at least the question becomes testable.

I have no problem with someone choosing to build on Sunday because they care about what they are making. But a salaried worker should also be able to do useful work, earn enough to live properly, and keep Sunday. If a boss wants more hours, say what those hours buy the worker: extra pay, a skill they can take elsewhere, a real promotion, a share in what they build? Asking for world-class commitment while keeping the reward vague is bullshit.

And then there are bonds. A company may genuinely spend money training someone. That does not mean every frightening exit number in an appointment letter is automatically owed. The [Supreme Court's 2025 Vijaya Bank judgment](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2014/40179/40179_2014_9_1503_61700_Judgement_14-May-2025.pdf) upheld a particular ₹2 lakh, three-year service condition in a public-bank recruitment setting. It did not turn every private employer's retention penalty into law. The point for a young worker is simpler: a bond can chill switching even before a court decides what is enforceable.<sup><a id="note-ref-bonds-1" href="#note-bonds" aria-label="Methodology note 6">6</a></sup>

## If you believe in markets, this should bother you

I like hard work. I like honest contracts. I like a firm that makes a profit because it earns a customer and keeps its promise. None of that requires me to applaud a salary headline that hides the guaranteed cash, an interview that measures prestige more than skill, or a billable night that disappears from the time sheet.

A functioning market lets the person selling their labour know the price, compare offers, leave a bad deal, and collect what the contract promises. Take away those conditions and “free market” becomes branding for whoever already has the lawyers.

This matters beyond a few office towers. [STPI's 2024–25 annual report](https://stpi.in/sites/default/files/annual-reports-documents/stpi_annual_report_2024_2025_eng.pdf) records **₹4,58,669.50 crore** in software exports by *STPI-registered units in Karnataka*. [Nasscom estimated](https://www.nasscom.in/sites/default/files/media_pdf/Nasscom%20SR%20Press%20release.pdf) **5.8 million people** in India's technology industry in FY2025. One is a Karnataka export figure for defined units; the other is a nationwide workforce estimate. Neither is a count of Bengaluru's software engineers.<sup><a id="note-ref-scale-1" href="#note-scale" aria-label="Methodology note 7">7</a></sup>

So when someone says, “Pay them more and the work will just move to Vietnam or the Philippines,” show me the actual contract. Some projects can move. Some already do. But a threat to move every job tomorrow has to account for client relationships, trained teams, and the systems that make delivery reliable. We just saw how small the direct client-rate change can be for a real raise in one plausible example.

The world has moved. Clients buy engineering judgement, cloud systems, products, and teams that can explain what went wrong when a release breaks. Even [Nasscom's review](https://www.nasscom.in/sites/default/files/media_pdf/Nasscom%20SR%20Press%20release.pdf) talks about growth in engineering R&D and digital engineering. If we respond by selling the same people as the cheapest available hours, we are training firms to compete by wearing out the talent that makes them valuable.

India cannot announce that it has moved beyond back-office work and then run its offices around somebody else's clock. The proof is in the working day: hours a team can plan, holidays people can actually take, benefits they can use, and a way to leave without putting the next job at risk. A new label on the export invoice changes none of that.

A foreign client is a customer and a partner, not a god. We can listen, make a promise, deliver the work, push back when the scope changes, and charge for urgent coverage. If a US release needs someone awake at midnight here, the firm can staff and pay a rotation. If it gives away one engineer's evening to win the bid, fuck that. The firm has to make this clear at the sales table; the worker should not have to negotiate it alone on a late call.

These terms change what security means. A worker with predictable time, usable health cover, savings, and a reasonable exit can learn, care for family, and take the next offer without begging for permission. Firms that price actual hours have a reason to keep skilled teams instead of treating exhaustion as a discount. That is professional value you can build a life around.

There is a cost to that model, even when it does not appear on the invoice. The worker pays it in late nights, a smaller bank balance, and time lost crossing the city. The employer gets to call the bid competitive. Bengaluru gets the traffic, the water demand, and the housing pressure. **The cheap bid did not remove the cost. It moved it onto the employee and the city.**

[Before Bengaluru Gets Any Bigger](https://justneeraj12.github.io/portfolio/writing/before-bengaluru-gets-bigger/) asks what the city gives back. A place can generate enormous export invoices while the people producing them lose hours reaching work, pay for tanker water, and rent whatever room the salary will bear. The employer did not create every broken street. The state still cannot celebrate the invoice and treat the worker's life outside the office as somebody else's balance sheet.

Tagore, writing about India's social divisions, put it bluntly: “Our real problem in India is not political. It is social.” [*Nationalism in India*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/40766/40766-h/40766-h.htm) That does not excuse the state from building roads, supplying water, or enforcing the law. It asks what kind of bargain we are making when everyone else pays for a cheap bid.

That is the digital zamindari I mean. Gatekeepers collect from a system they did not build alone. The person doing the work cannot even see the whole ledger. That ledger is where I would start changing the rules.

## Follow the money out of the office

Suppose **100,000 workers** on ₹6 lakh guaranteed cash move to ₹8 lakh. That is **₹2,000 crore a year in additional gross payroll**. It is an illustration, not a count of Bengaluru workers at that salary, and certainly not ₹2,000 crore automatically added to India's GDP. If overseas clients fund the increase through higher invoices, more foreign revenue comes in. If firms fund it from margins, the gain shifts from profits to wages. The route matters; the worker's claim on the value they create matters too.<sup><a id="note-ref-scale-3" href="#note-scale" aria-label="Methodology note 7">7</a></sup>

Now take one of those paychecks home. Some is withheld for tax or saved through PF. The rest can make rent less frightening, pay down a loan, buy better food, cover a parent's medicine, or become an emergency fund. I am not assigning a made-up spending percentage to every family. The point is that a regular wage creates choices a bonus headline cannot.

Those choices do not stop at the apartment door. A worker pays a neighbourhood mechanic or café. The café pays staff and buys ingredients; the grocer buys from a farmer or wholesaler; a purchase made in Bengaluru may support a manufacturer in another state. Some workers send money home. Some save for a course, a deposit, or a business they could not risk starting while one missed paycheck meant trouble.

Household spending is already more than half of India's economy, according to the [national accounts](https://mospi.gov.in/uploads/latestReleases/latest_release_1772189865181_f040336d-bc57-4aed-b80f-586d9ccb279e_Press_Note_on_New_Series_of_GDP_Estimates_with_Base_Year_2022-23_27022026.pdf). A better wage can feed that demand in Bengaluru and well beyond it. Some spending will also go to imports, and some of the gain will be saved. There is no honest one-number multiplier to attach to the raise.<sup><a id="note-ref-scale-6" href="#note-scale" aria-label="Methodology note 7">7</a></sup>

There is evidence for part of this path. A [World Bank study of Indian districts](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/099255006212237589/pdf/IDU058da6a290035604fc4081820c85c365125c4.pdf) found that growth in export-facing service jobs also increased employment in local services, with household demand a likely channel. That study measures **job growth**, not the effect of this proposed wage rise. It gives us a reason to take the spillover seriously, not a multiplier to paste onto ₹2,000 crore.<sup><a id="note-ref-scale-4" href="#note-scale" aria-label="Methodology note 7">7</a></sup>

> “An ideal society should be mobile, should be full of channels for conveying a change taking place in one part to other parts.”
>
> [B. R. Ambedkar, *Annihilation of Caste*, §14](https://ccnmtl.columbia.edu/projects/mmt/ambedkar/web/section_14.html)

He was arguing about caste and fraternity, not predicting a wage multiplier. But the question is useful here too: if one group gains, are there working channels through which anyone else can gain with them?

There may be more tax from taxable purchases and earnings, but it does not flow straight into a Bengaluru road or a public tap. Governments have to choose that investment.

Henry George put the other danger in a sentence: “That increasing productive power does not add to the reward of labor, is not because of competition, but because competition is one-sided.” [*Progress and Poverty*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/55308/55308-h/55308-h.htm) He was writing about land.

If housing stays scarce while demand rises, landlords can take much of the raise. The neighbourhood cleaner, driver, and shop worker can then face higher prices without a better wage. **A richer tech worker is not enough; the city has to become more liveable for everyone doing its work.** [World Bank on India's urban housing and services needs](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2025/07/22/india-has-a-critical-opportunity-to-drive-resilient-urban-development-says-new-world-bank-report)<sup><a id="note-ref-scale-5" href="#note-scale" aria-label="Methodology note 7">7</a></sup>

That is why I care about the ₹8 lakh target and the bus, the water line, the rental home, the mechanic, and the ITI graduate in the same argument. Better pay can help India keep talented people, give families resilience, and support businesses outside the tech park. It will do more of that when other workers can bargain for their share and public money makes the city work. Bengaluru is made every day by the people who keep it running, whether anyone puts their name on a tower or not.

## New York made the data answer back

New York's delivery workers did something Indian office workers could learn from. [Los Deliveristas Unidos organised](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/about/PublicComments-Proposed-Rule-Minimum-Pay-for-Food-Delivery-Workers-2022.pdf) for a pay standard. The city required app data, checked actual payment records, and enforced the rule.

In September 2026, New York announced a **$131.5 million DoorDash settlement**, including **more than $115 million in restitution** for over **260,000 workers**. Investigators analysed more than 152 million payment transactions and 110 million working hours. The city said the company had excluded categories of trip and on-call time from the minimum-pay calculation. [NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection](https://www.nyc.gov/site/dca/news/065-26/mayor-mamdani-wins-record-131-5-million-doordash-delivery-workers)<sup><a id="note-ref-newyork-1" href="#note-newyork" aria-label="Methodology note 9">9</a></sup>

An app courier and a software consultant have different jobs and different laws. The transferable idea is plain: write down the pay rule, preserve the records that test it, and give workers a way to challenge the result. If a firm can calculate a client's bill to the minute, a state can ask what happened to the worker's minute.

## Put the fair contract in writing

Here is what I would ask Karnataka to start, and Delhi to finish where national law is needed. Five changes. Each needs an office responsible for enforcement and a record the worker can actually use.

**First, stop selling CTC as cash.** Job ads and offer letters should lead with *guaranteed annual gross cash* and the monthly fixed-pay schedule. Put variable pay on its own line, with its conditions and past payout range if the company wants to advertise it. Put employer PF, gratuity provision, insurance, and other costs in another section. State who the health plan covers, its exclusions, and what happens to it when the job ends.

Then show an individual estimate of monthly bank credit after the worker's deductions and chosen tax declaration. Nobody can guarantee the same post-tax amount to every applicant. They can show the guaranteed cash before the candidate moves cities for it.

**Second, make overtime auditable.** Karnataka can enforce the hours and double-pay rule it already has. I would add a presumption that a worker earning below **₹24 lakh in guaranteed annual cash** is covered unless the employer can show real managerial authority. That number is my proposed threshold, not current law.<sup><a id="note-ref-overtime-3" href="#note-overtime" aria-label="Methodology note 5">5</a></sup>

Give workers written schedules and time records they can see. Put the working hours, weekly rest, the Indian team's holiday calendar, paid leave, and on-call rotation in the employment terms. Karnataka already provides paid leave for covered workers; a leave balance is useless if every request is met with a project emergency. Staff the client coverage so a team can actually take its days off.<sup><a id="note-ref-working-1" href="#note-working" aria-label="Methodology note 12">12</a></sup>

Correct missing hours promptly and protect people who challenge a record. When a dispute happens, check instructions, tickets, messages, VPN records, and client billing together. A green dot alone is not a time clock. National law should align the rule across states.

**Third, make public concessions buy decent work.** For *new* Karnataka-controlled land concessions, grants, and service contracts, require audited pay and billing disclosures. I would test a **35% worker-pay floor** on identifiable time-and-materials staff-augmentation contracts, measured against net labour revenue for the billed seat. Publish the costs and results before taking it further.<sup><a id="note-ref-concessions-1" href="#note-concessions" aria-label="Methodology note 10">10</a></sup>

A fixed-price product contract needs a different test, such as payroll share and recorded hours, or firms will just change the invoice label. Karnataka can attach conditions to its own future benefits. It cannot rewrite a Union SEZ tax exemption by itself. [SEZ India, incentives and sunset](https://sezindia.gov.in/FAQ)

**Fourth, make social security follow the person.** PF already has a permanent UAN and a transfer route between employers. Make that route actually work. Let a worker see deposits, balances, and claims without begging HR for an explanation.

For regular employees, replace gratuity's five-year cliff with funded, proportionate accrual that follows the worker. Fixed-term employees already have a different rule; keep that gain. Protect retirement saving while allowing sensible hardship access to a worker's own money. This part needs national legislation, not a speech at Vidhana Soudha. [EPFO transfer FAQ](https://www.epfindia.gov.in/site_docs/PDFs/UAN_PDFs/UAN_ForMembers/FAQ-Member-Ver1.1.pdf); [Code on Social Security, section 53](https://labour.gov.in/sites/default/files/ss_code_as_passed_by_lok_sabha.pdf)<sup><a id="note-ref-security-1" href="#note-security" aria-label="Methodology note 11">11</a></sup>

**Fifth, make the exit fair in both directions.** I would start junior roles with 30 days' notice on either side, a written handover, and pay in lieu if the employer ends the job earlier. Let people agree to an earlier exit without an arbitrary bond penalty. If a project cannot survive one person leaving, train a second person; do not hold the next offer hostage with a three-month notice period. Existing protections against dismissal still matter.<sup><a id="note-ref-working-2" href="#note-working" aria-label="Methodology note 12">12</a></sup>

None of these rules requires the state to set every engineer's salary. They require the employer to say what it will pay, account for the hours it uses, make benefits and leave usable, and honour the conditions attached to public help. If firms really compete for talent, they should be able to compete in daylight.

## Stop practising obedience

There is an individual part to this. Keep your offer letter. Ask what is fixed cash. Record the hours you actually work. Share pay ranges with colleagues where you can. Organise around a specific demand instead of merely complaining in the lift. A professional title should make it easier to speak plainly, not harder.

There is a civic part too. If Bengaluru's salaried class treats election day as a long weekend in Coorg, and an 11 p.m. call from a foreign client as a sacred obligation, we have our priorities upside down. We should be able to tell a client our hours, our holidays, and what extra coverage costs, then tell our own government which laws are not being enforced. That is how you deal with a peer. It is a more serious form of civic pride than another skyline photograph.

New York has its own housing and inequality problems. But its delivery workers organised, pressed for a rule, and gave the city something to enforce. That is a better lesson to take from a great city than staring at its towers.

Politicians can approve a project. Corporations can finance one. Neither is the city.

> “That is no city, which belongs to one man.”
>
> Haemon in [Sophocles, *Antigone*](https://beta.perseus.tufts.edu/urn%3Acts%3AgreekLit%3Atlg0011.tlg002.jebb1904-eng1%3A724/)

The mason lays the brick, the cleaner keeps the street usable, the driver gets someone to work, the engineer keeps the system running, and neighbours decide whether to demand better. People build the place and make its institutions answer to them. A ribbon-cutting photograph comes later.

Enough of being complacent and obedient. If the deal is “be grateful, work late, do not ask where the money went, and call the traffic progress,” **fuck that**. A worker should be able to build wealth and still have a life. A city should make that easier with housing, transport, water, and an actual voice in what happens next.

I still want Indian firms to win global work. I want them to win it because they are excellent, pay honestly, and help build a city people can live in. A country that claims meritocracy should let the person who earned the seat see the terms of the seat.

**India cannot build world-class cities or world-class industries by underpaying the hands that build them—whether they hold a welding torch in an ITI or a ThinkPad in an office.**

<p id="note-security"><strong>11.</strong> EPFO's UAN is permanent across jobs and supports transfer, though administrative failures can occur. The labour ministry's 2026 FAQ says a fixed-term employee qualifies after one year. Proportionate vesting for regular employees and any new hardship access are policy proposals, not current entitlements. <a href="https://www.epfindia.gov.in/site_docs/PDFs/UAN_PDFs/UAN_ForMembers/FAQ-Member-Ver1.1.pdf">EPFO UAN FAQ</a>; <a href="https://labour.gov.in/sites/default/files/ss_code_as_passed_by_lok_sabha.pdf">Code on Social Security</a>; <a href="https://www.labour.gov.in/static/uploads/2026/03/a4ccf4c6d97c4f1f36a6d83f8c64213d.pdf?v=20260620082854">ministry FAQ</a>. <a href="#note-ref-security-1" aria-label="Return to note 11 reference 1">↩</a></p>

<p id="note-working"><strong>12.</strong> The Karnataka Shops and Commercial Establishments Act provides annual paid leave and other paid leave in section 15 for covered employees. Its IT/ITeS exemption from section 12's weekly-holiday provision is specific; it does not erase section 15. Section 39 requires reasonable cause and one month's employer notice or pay in lieu for a covered employee after six months' continuous service, subject to its misconduct exception and other applicable terms. Coverage and any valid exemption should be checked for the particular workplace.
